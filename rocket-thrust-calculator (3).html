🎛️ Engine controls

🔥 Chamber pressure
How hard the hot gas pushes inside the engine. Raptor 3 is about 300 bar, roughly 300 times the air pressure around you!
🔔 Nozzle expansion ratio
How much wider the nozzle exit is than its narrowest point (the throat). Bigger bells let the gas expand more.
⛽ Propellant flow
How many kilograms of methane and liquid oxygen burn every second. The engine's throat is resized to match.

🟢 Try this in Kerbal Space Program 1

KSP 1 engines behave a lot like the calculator above: each one has two specific impulse numbers, one at sea level (1 atm) and one in vacuum, and its thrust changes as the air thins out. That's the same over- and underexpanded nozzle physics you see in the shock diamonds. Compare these stock engines with the Raptor 3 model. Kerbin's gravity is set to match Earth's (9.81 m/s²), so tons-force works the same way: TF = kN ÷ 9.81.
Bars: orange is sea-level Isp, purple is vacuum Isp. The black line is the Raptor 3's ~330 s at sea level. KSP numbers are the stock values listed on the official KSP wiki for KSP 1.x. (Some numbers floating around online are from older versions.)

🏆 Challenge: match or beat the Raptor 3

  1. Pick an engine from the cards above and build a simple rocket in the VAB: a command pod, a fuel tank big enough for the engine, and the engine.
  2. Check its numbers before launch. Hover over or right-click the engine in the parts list to read its thrust, Isp at sea level and in vacuum, and mass. Open the stage Δv panel in the bottom right, switch its situation between Kerbin sea level and vacuum, and watch the Isp and Δv change.
  3. Convert the thrust to tons-force (kN ÷ 9.81) and write both numbers in the table below.
  4. Sea-level round: can any stock engine beat the Raptor 3's ~330 s Isp at sea level? (Hint: look at the orange bars, then think about why.)
  5. Space round: which engines beat 330 s in vacuum? Now set the calculator's air pressure to Space. How much does the Raptor 3 model improve, and why does the Nerv still win?
  6. Lift-off check: your rocket only leaves the pad if thrust at sea level (kN) > total mass (t) × 9.81. That number is the thrust-to-weight ratio, and it needs to be above 1. Which engine can lift the heaviest rocket?
  7. Bonus: launch and watch the flame as you climb from the pad to space. When does it start to spread out? Try matching it to the calculator's Underexpanded and In space states.

Answer key for teachers: at sea level no stock KSP 1 engine reaches 330 s. The best are the Vector and Mammoth at 295 s. In vacuum, the Terrier (345 s), the Poodle (350 s) and especially the Nerv (800 s) beat it. Chemical engines like Raptor are limited by the energy in their propellant, while the Nerv heats its propellant with a nuclear reactor instead of burning it. That's why it can reach 800 s. The calculator's Raptor 3 sea-level nozzle reaches about s in vacuum. SpaceX's separate Raptor Vacuum, with a much bigger bell, does better.

How it works: this uses the ideal rocket equations. Thrust = (propellant flow × exhaust speed) + (exit pressure − outside pressure) × exit area. Exhaust gas is treated with γ = 1.2 and a characteristic velocity of 1,850 m/s, typical for methane and oxygen, which makes the Raptor 3 baseline come out at about 2,750 kN and 330 s. KSP 1 engine stats are stock values from the official KSP wiki. Real engines lose a little to heat, friction and imperfect burning, and the shock-diamond picture is a simplified drawing, not a fluid simulation.
